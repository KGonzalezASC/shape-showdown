# Legal-risk checklist for a falling-block-inspired browser game (U.S.)

**Research date:** 2026-07-30  
**Scope:** General U.S.-oriented research for Shape Showdown, not legal advice or a clearance opinion. Results depend on the jurisdiction, claims pleaded, product versions, evidence of copying, and the exact visual/audio presentation.

## Bottom line

Using a falling-block puzzle concept and general game rules is materially lower copyright risk than copying the source game’s audiovisual presentation. It is not a complete safe harbor. In *Tetris Holding, LLC v. Xio Interactive, Inc.*, the District of New Jersey held that the abstract Tetris idea and rules were unprotectable, but granted summary judgment for Tetris on copyright and federal trade dress because *Mino* copied the overall visual look and feel while admitting deliberate copying. The opinion was a fact-specific district-court decision, not a universal rule that all similar mechanics infringe.

The safest product posture is independent implementation plus a visibly original visual system, audio set, brand, and marketing presentation. Unique mechanics and custom visuals reduce similarity and confusion risk; they do not immunize a product that still reproduces protected expression or suggests authorization.

## What the 2012 opinion actually held

The court treated the following as the general, abstract idea/rules of Tetris and therefore not copyrightable: manipulating geometric square-block pieces that fall, rotating/fitting them, completing horizontal lines, earning points/clearing lines, and losing when pieces reach the top. This follows the Copyright Act’s idea/expression boundary. ([Opinion, pp. 8–9, 24–25](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115/pdf/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115-0.pdf); [Copyright Office Circular 33](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ33.pdf); [17 U.S.C. ch. 1](https://www.copyright.gov/title17/92chap1.html))

But the court found protectable expression, in the context of the works’ overwhelming similarity, in the pieces’ style, design, shape, movement, rotation, bright colors, delineated component squares, interior borders, shading/gradation, exact 20-by-10 field dimensions, garbage lines, ghost pieces, next-piece preview, lock-color change, and board-filling game-over presentation. The court emphasized that the individual elements might not independently establish infringement; the problem was the wholesale copying of the Tetris look. ([Opinion, pp. 26–33](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115/pdf/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115-0.pdf))

The court rejected the argument that anything related to game functionality is automatically outside copyright. Its analysis turned on whether alternative expressive choices were practically available; it found many alternatives here. It also rejected merger and scènes-à-faire on these facts because this was a fanciful puzzle presentation with no stock imagery that had to be used. ([Opinion, pp. 18–19, 24, 29–30](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115/pdf/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115-0.pdf))

For trade dress, the court considered Tetris’s claimed combination of brightly colored four-square pieces and a tall rectangular playfield. It stated the federal elements as distinctiveness/secondary meaning, nonfunctionality, and likelihood of consumer confusion, and found the claimed choices nonfunctional because many alternative designs would work. It also explained that the claim addressed confusion from packaging/advertising, rather than simply converting every copyright allegation into a Lanham Act claim. ([Opinion, pp. 35–38](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115/pdf/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115-0.pdf))

The court did **not** decide every alleged Tetris feature as a matter of law, did **not** hold that Tetris owns the general falling-block concept, and did **not** decide the remaining state-law counts on the motions before it. The result also rested on undisputed facts, including Xio’s purposeful copying and concession of substantial similarities. ([Opinion, pp. 1–2, 7–8, 34–35](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115/pdf/USCOURTS-njd-3_09-cv-06115-0.pdf))

## Checklist

| Issue | Assessment | Practical risk control |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideas and game mechanics** | General rules, procedures, methods, and systems are outside copyright. The *Tetris* opinion specifically left the abstract falling-piece/line-clearing rules unprotected. Patent, contract, trademark, and other claims are separate questions. | Use mechanics at the abstract level, but make meaningful independent choices about piece families, movement/rotation behavior, board dimensions, scoring, effects, progression, and presentation. Keep design notes showing independent development. |
| **Copyright in the game as a whole** | A browser game can contain protectable code, artwork, animations, text, audiovisual material, and other original expression. Copyright risk rises when the product reproduces the source game’s total concept and feel, even without copying source code or exact image files. ([Copyright Office, Performing Arts and audiovisual works](https://www.copyright.gov/registration/performing-arts/)) | Create or commission original assets; do not trace, extract, recolor, or closely imitate reference screens, animations, icons, copy, or layout. Review the complete play flow, not just isolated sprites. |
| **Trade dress / board and UI presentation** | Trade dress is fact-specific. USPTO guidance recognizes product design/packaging trade dress can include size, shape, color combinations, texture, and graphics, subject to functionality and distinctiveness. The *Tetris* court found its claimed color/piece/field combination nonfunctional and found confusion on the undisputed record. This does not mean every rectangular playfield or preview widget is proprietary. | Avoid the source game’s exact board proportions/dimensions, piece styling, color treatment, previews, ghost/garbage presentation, lock/game-over states, surrounding chrome, store imagery, and promotional composition as a combined package. Test whether an ordinary viewer could mistake the game for an official version. |
| **Trademarks and branding** | Names, logos, stylized lettering, slogans, and commercial impressions can create trademark/false-endorsement risk even when gameplay is different. Similarity in sound, appearance, meaning, or overall commercial impression can matter when goods/services are related. “Inspired by” wording is not a defense to a confusing brand. | Keep **Shape Showdown** and its logo/voice clearly distinct; do not use Tetris names, logos, trade dress, “official,” “authorized,” or lookalike store/marketing language. Run a comprehensive clearance search of USPTO, state, and internet uses before launch. ([USPTO likelihood of confusion](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/likelihood-confusion); [USPTO clearance guidance](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/search/comprehensive-clearance-search-similar-trademarks)) |
| **Music and sound** | Music and recordings are separate copyright works: a composition (music/lyrics) and a sound recording (a particular fixation/performance). The *Tetris* opinion mentioned music/sounds in cited precedent but did not need to decide all of Tetris’s audio allegations. A recognizable melody, sample, recording, or distinctive sound effect can create an independent claim. | Use original audio, public-domain material after checking the applicable term, or a license that expressly covers browser distribution, streaming, synchronization/game use, edits, territories, and commercial use. Obtain written assignments/licenses from composers, performers, and sound designers as needed; do not assume a “royalty-free” label covers all rights. ([Copyright Office music guidance](https://www.copyright.gov/register/pa-sr.html); [Copyright Office permission guidance](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/m10.pdf)) |
| **Color mapping** | There is no blanket rule that a party owns ordinary colors or every assignment of color to a piece. USPTO guidance says color marks generally are not inherently distinctive and usually require acquired distinctiveness, but color combinations can form part of trade dress. Separately, the *Tetris* court treated bright colors plus the copied piece style as part of protectable expression on its facts. | Do not reproduce a source game’s exact palette-to-piece mapping together with its shapes, shading, borders, movement, and UI. Choose a different palette and visual grammar; treat the whole combination—not color in isolation—as the risk object. |
| **Unique mechanics, branding, and custom visuals** | These are meaningful risk reducers because they make independent expression and a distinct commercial impression easier to show. They are not legal guarantees: a game can add novel mechanics while retaining a confusing or substantially similar presentation. | Make originality visible at first glance and throughout play: distinct pieces, proportions, animation language, effects, HUD, typography, audio, name, logo, store art, and copy. Perform a side-by-side review against likely references before release. |

## Recommended release gate

Before public launch, have counsel familiar with U.S. game/IP law review (1) a side-by-side capture of representative gameplay and menus, (2) the name/logo and store listing, (3) music/sound licenses and contributor agreements, and (4) any third-party art, fonts, libraries, or purchased asset licenses. Keep the review jurisdiction-specific; a New Jersey federal district-court opinion from 2012 is useful guidance but does not answer every claim or every country’s law.

This note is research, not a legal opinion. It does not determine whether any particular asset, mechanic, mark, or release is lawful.
